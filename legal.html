In memory · Sinclair industrial design

Rick Dickinson

The shape of a computer. A memory for life.

Rick Dickinson was the industrial designer responsible for the exterior of the Sinclair QL, introduced in 1984. Its case is explicitly credited to Dickinson by the Centre for Computing History. In this emulator, recognition of his work has a special place.

His contribution deserves to be understood in its full breadth. In his interviews about design, Dickinson explained how a product’s shape also emerges from ergonomics, materials, component layout and manufacturing requirements. His QL studies show that care in giving a coherent form to an ambitious technical idea.

The memory we want to preserve

The QL set out to innovate technically. Yet seeing a Sinclair QL in a computer shop window in 1985 is one of those images that stays with you for life. Its presence, its lines, the promise of everything you could do with it. The ZX Spectrum + and the ZX81 / Timex Sinclair 1000 hold the same place in our affections: computers whose shapes still awaken the curiosity and wonder of that time.

Dickinson also designed the ZX81 and ZX Spectrum, as he recalls in the interview about his career. The Timex Sinclair 1000 was the North American version of the ZX81, as documented by the Smithsonian. Preserving these computers also means remembering the people who shaped them and recognising the place of industrial design in their history.

A personal recollection

I exchanged messages with Rick about once a year. I tried not to take advantage of his kindness, which I greatly appreciated.

As fate would have it, I sent him a message without knowing he was ill, let alone that his illness was terminal. In that message, I told him how much I admired his designs, which I saw as works of modern art, singling out the Sinclair QL in particular.

As far as I recall, Rick passed away that same day or the following day. I treasure the memory of having expressed that admiration to him, without imagining how close the farewell was.

Luís Simões da Cunha · author of this emulator

Thank you, Rick, for your talent, your care and the many memories that stay with us.

Special dedication · ZX81 and Sinclair QL

Dr Carlo Delhez

More possibilities for a machine. More time for its history.

This is a tribute to the genius and dedication of Carlo Delhez. In a Sinclair world so often remembered for the ZX Spectrum, his work gave the ZX81 and QL tools to keep creating, experimenting and surprising us. His programs connected machines, generations of users and two ways of preserving computing: extending what a computer can do and keeping its software alive.

In the XTricator manual, Delhez recounts buying his first ZX81 in 1982, learning Z80 machine code with the help of Toni Baker’s book and acquiring a QL in 1987. He describes himself as holding a doctorate in Physics and explains what led him to emulation: extending the life of the ZX81 as he saw its user base decline. [1]

ZX81 · from 1987

Coral BASIC: a new way to program

The Coral Basic Interpreter (CBI) brought the ZX81 an editor in which command words are typed letter by letter, with immediate syntax checking. Version 7.0 documents 43 new commands in around 5 KB of code: procedures with data passed in both directions, structured loops, conditional blocks with ELSE, DATA, READ and RESTORE.

It also added renumbering, execution tracing and facilities for integrating machine code routines. On a computer with such limited resources, this was a remarkable extension of programming freedom. The manual identifies Terminal Software in the Netherlands as the first commercial publisher and dates the Dutch manual to 1987. [2]

ZX81 · 1991 revision

ZX-Assembler-2: tools to go deeper

The archives identify Delhez’s assembler as ZX-Assembler-2, with the filename ZXASSEM2. It was a revision of ZX-Assembler by D. P. Aknai e M. Streeton, published by Artic Computing in 1982. Crediting that origin is part of recognising Delhez’s work accurately.

The description he wrote for his program library records a machine code version held in line 0, with source code in line 2 and generated code in line 1. It added the ORGdirective, allowing the origin of the generated code to be set. It was an ingenious way of organising a development tool within the ZX81’s memory structure. [3]

PC · MS-DOS

XTender and XTender2: preserving behaviour too

XTender brought the ZX81 to PCs, including XT, AT, 386 and 486 machines. The preserved version 1.22 package includes Coral BASIC, ZX-Assembler-2, demonstrations and documentation: a small environment for using and programming the ZX81 again. [4]

With XTender2, attention to the machine’s behaviour became particularly thorough. The beta 11 documentation, from May 2002, describes video system emulation at the level of individual Z80 clock cycles, memory and ROM configurations, FAST and SLOWmodes, high-resolution graphics, virtual cassette and multiple simultaneous keypresses.

It even included a television’s response to irregular synchronisation signals: the picture could shake, flicker or roll as it did on real equipment. That detail conveys the ambition: to preserve the experience of sitting in front of the machine as well. [4]

Sinclair QL · first official release in January 1992

XTricator: a ZX81 inside the QL

Written in assembly, XTricator ran ZX81 software on QDOS systems. Version 1.75, from August 1994, offered high-resolution graphics, ZX Printer emulation, file access through QZ-DOS and integration with the Pointer Interface for multitasking.

Coral BASIC and ZX-Assembler-2 could occupy the area between 8 and 16 KB of the emulated machine. The QL thus became a place where the ZX81 could continue to be used as a creative tool. The manual also acknowledges the speed limitations on unaccelerated QLs. [1]

Confirmed · ZX Spectrum on the Sinclair QL

Spectator: yes, the Spectrum too

Delhez also wrote Spectator, a Spectrum emulator for the QL. Its preliminary version 0.56 manual, from March 1992, describes a 48 KB ZX Spectrum environment with the Interface 1 ROM, Z80 code execution, keyboard, screen and routing of tape, Microdrive and serial port operations.

The program supported multitasking and was also intended for Minerva. Delhez was clear about the cost of emulation: that version was slow on an original QL and benefited from a Gold Card. Its merit lies in its documented technical ambition, with limitations that the author himself was careful to explain. [5]

There was also the patient work of making programs accessible. The library Delhez documented in December 1993 brought together 240 programs on three discs, accompanied by usage descriptions. Creating tools and preserving the knowledge needed to use them were parts of the same dedication. [6]

Thank you, Carlo, for finding so many possibilities in these small machines and helping others discover them. This emulator pays tribute to that ingenuity and that determination to keep curiosity alive.

History · A collective creation

The people behind the QL

Behind every circuit, command and line of the case, there were people.

Getting to know the QL also means getting to know those who gave it form, behaviour and possibilities. This acknowledgement brings together contributions to the machine and its expansion; it is not intended as a complete list of everyone involved in its history.

Karlin recalls an organisation without a single conventional project manager: hardware, software and design each had their own leads. The attributions below distinguish personal recollections from documentary records. [1]

David Karlin

Lead hardware and electronics designer

Worked on the electronics, ZX8301 and ZX8302 custom circuits and printed circuit board, and liaised with manufacturers. [1]

Tony Tebby

QDOS · Operating system and firmware

As the author of QDOS, he gave the QL an architecture with pre-emptive multitasking: the system shares processor time between programs. The technical guide he co-authored with Karlin documents jobs, their priorities and scheduling — mechanisms that help explain the ambition of this 1984 computer. [2] [3]

Jan Jones

SuperBASIC

Wrote the BASIC interpreter; according to Karlin, she was recruited by Tebby. [1] SuperBASIC is both a structured programming language and the command environment through which the user communicates with the QL. [4]

Rick Dickinson

Industrial design · Case and keyboard

His work covered drawings, models, prototypes and injection moulding specifications for the case and keyboard. [1] Our special tribute to Rick recalls the enduring presence of that design in the memories of those who knew these machines.

Sir Clive Sinclair

Product vision and executive leadership

Chaired decision-making meetings. Karlin recalls, however, differences of opinion about the professional market the QL was intended to serve. [1] It was Sinclair who publicly unveiled the machine on 12 January 1984. [2]

Jim Westwood

Engineering laboratory management

Ran the laboratory. According to Karlin, he, Tebby and Dickinson formally reported to Westwood, in a structure that operated quite informally. [1]

David Southward

Microdrive technology

Karlin explicitly credits him with the Microdrives, the storage system built into the QL. [1]

Ben Cheese

Electronics engineering · Microdrive hardware

Sinclair’s internal directory identifies him as an electronics engineer developing Microdrive hardware. [5] Karlin highlights the importance of his collaboration. [1]

John Mathieson

Hardware · Hard disc interface

The Sinclair QL Preservation Project credits him with a hard disc interface created in 1983 and demonstrated at the QL launch, which never became a commercial product. It also records his later involvement in developing the Atari Jaguar. [2]

Jonathan Oakley

Hardware engineering

The Sinclair QL Preservation Project identifies him among the additional hardware engineers. The source does not single out a circuit or subsystem; his name equally deserves to be associated with the collective work. [2]

Richard Miller

Hardware and expansion · Floppy disc interface

Included in the preservation project’s hardware credits, he developed a floppy disc interface for the QL at Micro Peripherals in 1985, later sold under the Sinclair brand. This contribution belongs to the machine’s expansion after launch. [2]

Aaron Turner

IPC firmware

Credited by the Sinclair QL Preservation Project for the firmware of the IPC, the subsystem based on the Intel 8049. This is a specific acknowledgement of that controller’s internal software. [2]

Nigel Searle

Company management and commercial decisions

Sinclair’s managing director, he took part in meetings and decisions; Karlin associates him with a significant role in commercial choices. [1]

Privacy and chat

ROMs and cartridges are processed in the browser. The application includes no advertising or analytics. It stores preferences, guide progress and cartridge protection choices locally. Projects you choose to save, including cartridge contents, also stay in the browser. You can delete them in the manager or in site data; first export any cartridges you want to keep. Visible chat history disappears when you reload the page.

To use Gemini, each user is responsible for obtaining their own API key through their account in Google AI Studio. This project does not provide a shared key. The key must be configured on the local server, following the setup instructions, and kept private; it must not be published or included in browser code. The local demo does not require a key.
